MarsNavigator

A 3D planner for safe rover and astronaut routes across Mars. Every data value comes from NASA Mars Trek (trek.nasa.gov).

React + CesiumJSJava 21 + Spring BootA* and DijkstraNASA Mars TrekRender + Vercel

1. Problem and solution in one paragraph

A safe path across Mars depends on more than relief: steep slopes stop a rover, thick dust clogs wheels and suits, loose ground traps you, and surface temperature changes with the hour of departure. We merged these indicators from NASA Mars Trek layers into a single traversability model on a hexagonal grid and route across it with A*. The result is shown on an interactive 3D globe of Mars: the route, its elevation profile, analysis layers and a first-person view.

How Trek data gets into the model

  1. Grid: 37,648 hexagons of about 1° (roughly 59 km) covering the western hemisphere. Slope per cell was computed by our team.
  2. Import (Java, TrekLayerImporter): the backend downloads Trek tiles — 98 tiles across MOLA, TES thermal inertia and TES dust — averages the pixels inside each cell, and saves the result to trek_layers.csv, so start-up needs no network.
  3. Image to number: MOLA height is read from the grey level (linear from −8,201 to +21,229 m). Dust and thermal inertia are read from the rainbow hue: violet is the minimum, red the maximum. Grey and transparent pixels count as no data.
  4. Sanity check: the highest cell is the summit of Olympus Mons (21,229 m), Tharsis is among the dustiest areas (index about 0.9), and the floor of Valles Marineris lies low.

3. Traversability model

Mobility profiles

ProfileSlope limitDustMinimum ground temperatureSpeed
Rover15° (slope + 2σ)never blocks, adds a penalty−95 °C150 m per sol
Astronaut25° (slope + 2σ)closed above index 0.70−80 °C6 km per sol

Cost of a step to a neighbouring cell

cost = great-circle distance on Mars × f
f = 1 + A·(climb/θmax)² + B·(descent/θmax)² + λ·σ/θmax + Wdust·dust² + Wloose·looseness + Wcold·cold
  • Climb and descent come from the MOLA height difference between cells. Climbing is penalised more than descending.
  • Slope σ: rough ground costs more even when the mean slope is small.
  • Looseness comes from TES thermal inertia: the lower the inertia, the more sand and dust.
  • A cell is closed when slope + 2σ is above the limit, dust is above the limit (astronaut), the ground is colder than the minimum at the departure hour, or there is no data.

Surface temperature (simplified model)

T(hour) = 215 K − 50 K·sin²(latitude) + (20 K + 60 K·(1 − inertia)) · cos(2π·(hour − 13)/24)

Loose ground with low thermal inertia heats up fast by day and cools hard at night. That is why the route depends on departure time: some cells close at night and open by day.

This is a model, not a direct measurement. Dust and inertia values are decoded from Trek tile colours, because Trek publishes no numeric legends for those tiles. Say so openly at the defence.

Algorithm

  • A* over the hex graph, six neighbours per cell. The heuristic is great-circle distance to the goal. It never overestimates the real cost (the factor f ≥ 1), so A* always finds the optimal path.
  • Dijkstra is the same search without the heuristic. A compare button in the app shows both find the same path while A* expands noticeably fewer cells, animated on the map.
  • Example: rover from Tharsis (110° W, 20° N) to Valles Marineris (60° W, 5° S): 3,857 km, A* expanded 4,497 cells. Astronaut from Viking 1 to Mars Pathfinder: 950 km.
  • Nearest open ground: if the start or goal is in a closed area, a breadth-first search over neighbours finds the closest cell open to that profile.

4. What the app does

  • 3D globe of Mars with real MOLA relief, exaggeration up to 10×
  • “Natural” (true colour) and “Elevation” (colour by height) base maps
  • Search for places and landing sites; a card for any point with elevation, slope, dust, thermal inertia, temperature and passability for both profiles
  • Route A → B for rover or astronaut; a clear reason when no route exists; one click to move a point to open ground
  • Route stats: length, ascent, max slope, travel time in sols, dust, temperature, risk; profile of elevation, dust and temperature along the way
  • Trek layers (dust, thermal inertia) and backend analysis layers with legends
  • Sol and time-of-day sliders: Sun position and the effect of departure hour on the route
  • First-person drive along the route; free walking with WASD where the model stops you at closed ground

7. Likely jury questions

Where does the data come from? Is it real measurement?

All layers come from NASA Mars Trek: MOLA elevation, dust and thermal inertia from the TES instrument, Viking and CTX imagery. Slopes were computed by our team on the grid. Dust and inertia are decoded from tile colours, so the relative ordering is accurate while absolute values are approximate.

Why A* and not just Dijkstra?

A* finds the same optimal path but expands fewer cells, because the heuristic steers the search toward the goal. The heuristic is admissible: great-circle distance never exceeds the real cost, since the cost factor is at least 1. The compare button in the app shows this.

Why can’t an astronaut cross Olympus Mons?

TES data puts the dust index on Tharsis and Olympus at about 0.85–0.94, while the astronaut limit is 0.70: thick dust clogs suits and equipment. The rover can pass, with a penalty. “Move to nearest open ground” finds the closest reachable point.

How does time of day matter?

Through a simplified surface-temperature model based on thermal inertia: loose ground gets very cold at night. When the temperature falls below the profile limit, that cell is closed at that hour, so the same two points give different routes for different departure times.

How precise is it? Why are the cells so large?

A 1° grid (about 59 km) is the strategic level, for planning over hundreds of kilometres. For a precise approach to a landing site the same model runs on a finer grid: the code does not depend on cell size.

Why only the western hemisphere?

Our slope grid currently covers longitudes −180° to 0°. The map and Trek layers work on the whole planet; routes there arrive as soon as the second half of the grid is ready.

How is this better than just viewing Mars Trek?

Trek shows layers one at a time. We combine them into one traversability model per profile and hour, compute the optimal route, explain why it looks the way it does, and let you travel it in 3D.

What is next?

The eastern hemisphere and a finer grid, calibrated dust and temperature values, rocks and radiation (the model already has slots for them), and route export to GeoJSON.
